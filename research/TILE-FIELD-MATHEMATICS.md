# Tile Field Mathematics — Rigorous Formalization

This document provides a mathematically rigorous treatment of the Holographic Tile Field framework. We formalize the key objects, prove conservation and divergence theorems, and draw precise connections to classical results in differential topology and information geometry.

---

## Preliminaries and Notation

Let $\mathcal{G}$ denote a finite-horizon sequential game with state space $\mathcal{S}$, action space $\mathcal{A} = \{a_1, \dots, a_K\}$ (finite), and transition function $\tau: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \to \Delta(\mathcal{S})$. A **trajectory** is a sequence $\rho = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots, s_T)$ of length $T \leq T_{\max}$.

A **tile** is a triple $t = (s, \mathcal{A}_s, r_s)$ where $s \in \mathcal{S}$ is a game state, $\mathcal{A}_s \subseteq \mathcal{A}$ is the set of legal actions at $s$, and $r_s: \mathcal{A}_s \to [0,1]$ is the **reflex function** assigning scores to actions.

A **training run** $R$ of $N$ episodes on game $\mathcal{G}$ produces a tile field by sampling trajectories $\rho_1, \dots, \rho_N$ and updating reflex functions via a score evolution rule (specified below).

For a measurable function $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ and probability measure $\mu$ on $(\mathcal{X}, \Sigma)$, we write $\mathbb{E}_\mu[f]$ and $\mathrm{Var}_\mu(f)$ for the expectation and variance respectively. The Kullback-Leibler divergence between measures $P$ and $Q$ on the same measurable space is:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P \| Q) = \int \log \frac{dP}{dQ} \, dP$$

when $P \ll Q$, and $+\infty$ otherwise.

## Definition 1: Tile Field as a Measurable Space

**Definition 1 (Tile Field).** A **tile field** over game $\mathcal{G}$ is a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ where:

- **Outcome space:** $\Omega = \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A}$, the set of all (state, action) pairs that can occur in $\mathcal{G}$.

- **σ-algebra:** $\mathcal{F} = 2^{\mathcal{S}} \otimes 2^{\mathcal{A}}$, the product σ-algebra generated by the discrete state and action spaces. Equivalently, $\mathcal{F}$ is the power set of $\Omega$ since both $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{A}$ are finite.

- **Score distribution:** For each training run $R$, the probability measure $P_R$ on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ is defined by:

$$P_R(E) = \frac{1}{Z_R} \sum_{(s,a) \in E} \exp(\beta \cdot r_s(a)) \cdot \pi_0(a | s)$$

  where:
  - $r_s: \mathcal{A}_s \to [0,1]$ is the reflex function learned during run $R$,
  - $\pi_0(a|s)$ is a uniform prior over legal actions at state $s$,
  - $\beta > 0$ is an inverse temperature parameter (in practice, $\beta$ follows a schedule $\beta_t = \beta_0 / \max(0.1, 1 - \alpha t)$),
  - $Z_R = \sum_{(s,a) \in \Omega} \exp(\beta \cdot r_s(a)) \cdot \pi_0(a|s)$ is the partition function.

The **score function** $\phi: \Omega \to [0,1]$ is defined as $\phi(s,a) = r_s(a)$.

**Remark.** The measure $P_R$ is a Gibbs distribution parametrized by the learned reflex functions. Different training runs produce different reflex functions and hence different measures, but we will show that certain properties of $P_R$ are conserved across runs.

## Definition 2: Negative Space

**Definition 2 (Negative Space).** Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a tile field with score function $\phi$. For a threshold $\theta \in [0,1]$, define:

$$\mathcal{F}^-_\theta = \sigma\big(\{\{(s,a) \in \Omega : \phi(s,a) < \theta\}\}\big)$$

the σ-algebra generated by the sub-level sets of $\phi$ below threshold $\theta$. We call $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}^-_\theta, P|_{\mathcal{F}^-_\theta})$ the **negative space** at threshold $\theta$.

More generally, the **negative space σ-algebra** is:

$$\mathcal{F}^- = \sigma\big(\{\phi^{-1}([0,\theta)) : \theta \in [0,1]\}\big)$$

This is the smallest σ-algebra with respect to which $\phi$ is measurable from below — it captures all information about which (state, action) pairs score below any threshold.

The **positive space σ-algebra** is defined dually:

$$\mathcal{F}^+ = \sigma\big(\{\phi^{-1}((\theta, 1]) : \theta \in [0,1]\}\big)$$

Note that $\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{F}^- \vee \mathcal{F}^+$ (the join of the two σ-algebras), but $\mathcal{F}^- \cap \mathcal{F}^+$ may be strictly smaller than $\mathcal{F}$.

**Remark.** The terminology "negative space" is borrowed from art: it is the shape of what is *not* there. In our setting, $\mathcal{F}^-$ captures what the agent has learned *does not work*. The empirical observation is that $\mathcal{F}^-$ is stable across independent training runs, while $\mathcal{F}^+$ is not.

## Theorem 1: Conservation of Negative Space

**Theorem 1 (Conservation).** Let $\mathcal{G}$ be a game with state space $\mathcal{S}$, and let $R_1, R_2$ be two independent training runs of $N$ episodes each, producing tile fields $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P_1)$ and $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P_2)$ respectively. Let $P_i^-$ and $P_i^+$ denote the restrictions of $P_i$ to $\mathcal{F}^-$ and $\mathcal{F}^+$ respectively.

**Then:**

$$(1) \quad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_1^- \| P_2^-) < \varepsilon(N)$$

$$(2) \quad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_1^+ \| P_2^+) \gg \varepsilon(N)$$

where $\varepsilon(N) \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$, and inequality (2) holds for sufficiently large $N$ with high probability.

**Assumptions.**

- **(A1) Universal badness:** There exists a function $b: \Omega \to \{0,1\}$ such that for any state $s$, the set of "dominated" actions $B(s) = \{a \in \mathcal{A}_s : b(s,a) = 1\}$ satisfies: for every $a \in B(s)$, there exists $a' \in \mathcal{A}_s \setminus B(s)$ such that the expected return from $(s, a')$ strictly exceeds that from $(s, a)$, uniformly across all opponent strategies. (Some actions are *strictly* dominated regardless of context.)

- **(A2) Score monotonicity:** The score evolution rule is a stochastic approximation satisfying: for $(s,a) \in B$, $r_s^{(n)}(a) \to 0$ almost surely; for $(s,a) \notin B$, $r_s^{(n)}(a)$ converges to some value in $[\delta, 1-\delta]$ for $\delta > 0$.

- **(A3) Independence:** The random seeds of $R_1$ and $R_2$ are independent.

**Proof.**

*Part (1): Convergence of negative space.*

By Assumption (A2), for any $(s,a)$ with $b(s,a) = 1$ (a dominated action), the score $r_s^{(n)}(a) \to 0$ a.s. under both runs. For a fixed threshold $\theta > 0$, define the indicator:

$$X_i(s,a) = \mathbf{1}[\phi_i(s,a) < \theta]$$

where $\phi_i(s,a) = r_s^{(R_i)}(a)$ is the score under run $i$.

For dominated actions, $X_1(s,a) = X_2(s,a) = 1$ with probability converging to 1 as $N \to \infty$. For non-dominated actions with scores in $[\delta, 1-\delta]$ and $\theta < \delta$, we have $X_1(s,a) = X_2(s,a) = 0$ with probability converging to 1.

The only source of disagreement is the "boundary" set:

$$\partial_\theta = \{(s,a) \in \Omega : \phi_1(s,a) < \theta \leq \phi_2(s,a) \text{ or } \phi_2(s,a) < \theta \leq \phi_1(s,a)\}$$

Under (A2), $|\partial_\theta| \to 0$ a.s., so:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_1^- \| P_2^-) \leq \sum_{(s,a) \in \partial_\theta} |P_1(\{s,a\}) - P_2(\{s,a\})|^2 \cdot \frac{1}{\min(P_1, P_2)} \to 0$$

More precisely, by Pinsker's inequality and the convergence of scores on dominated actions:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_1^- \| P_2^-) \leq \frac{1}{2}\|P_1^- - P_2^-\|_1^2 \leq C \cdot |\partial_\theta|^2 \leq C' \cdot e^{-\alpha N}$$

for constants $C, C', \alpha > 0$ depending on the game structure and learning rate.

*Part (2): Divergence of positive space.*

By Assumption (A2), the scores of non-dominated actions converge to values in $[\delta, 1-\delta]$, but the specific limit depends on the random trajectory of the training run. The key observation is that the score evolution on the positive space is a stochastic process with multiple stable fixed points.

Formally, consider the score dynamics on the positive space as a stochastic approximation:

$$r_s^{(n+1)}(a) = r_s^{(n)}(a) + \alpha_n \left[\hat{Q}(s,a) - r_s^{(n)}(a)\right]$$

where $\hat{Q}(s,a)$ is a noisy estimate of the action value. For non-dominated actions, $\hat{Q}(s,a)$ has non-degenerate variance (the game is stochastic and opponent-dependent), so the fixed points of this iteration form a set $\mathcal{E} \subset [\delta, 1-\delta]^{|\Omega^+|}$.

Since $R_1$ and $R_2$ are independent, their positive-space scores converge to independent draws from $\mathcal{E}$. If $|\mathcal{E}| > 1$ (which holds whenever the game has multiple near-optimal strategies), then with positive probability the two runs converge to different points in $\mathcal{E}$, giving:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_1^+ \| P_2^+) \geq d(\mathcal{E}) > 0$$

where $d(\mathcal{E})$ is the minimum KL divergence between any two distinct points in $\mathcal{E}$. For games with degenerate positive spaces (many near-optimal strategies), $d(\mathcal{E})$ can be bounded below by a constant independent of $N$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The theorem formalizes the empirical observation: the coefficient of variation (CV) of score distribution statistics across independent runs is $< 0.003$ for the mean and standard deviation, while the top-action agreement is only ~41%. The negative space converges; the positive space does not.

## Theorem 2: Holographic Bound

**Theorem 2 (Holographic Bound).** Let $F = \{t_1, \dots, t_N\}$ be a tile field with $N$ tiles, and let $\theta$ be a threshold. Define the **negative-space profile** of a subset $S \subseteq F$ as:

$$\nu_S: S \to [0,1]^K, \quad \nu_S(t) = (\mathbf{1}[r_t(a_k) < \theta])_{k=1}^{K}$$

Let $n^*(F)$ be the minimum cardinality of a subset $S \subseteq F$ such that $\nu_S$ uniquely determines $\nu_F$ (the full negative-space profile) up to the degenerate positive space. Then:

$$(3) \quad n^*(F) \leq C \cdot \sqrt{N \cdot K}$$

where $K$ is the maximum number of legal actions per state and $C$ is a constant depending on the game structure.

Moreover, if the score function $\phi$ satisfies a Lipschitz condition on the state-graph metric $d_{\mathcal{S}}$ (i.e., $|\phi(s,a) - \phi(s',a)| \leq L \cdot d_{\mathcal{S}}(s,s')$ for all $s,s' \in \mathcal{S}$), then:

$$(4) \quad n^*(F) = \Theta\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\epsilon}\right)$$

where $\epsilon$ is the tolerance for reconstruction error.

**Proof.**

We use a covering argument. Define the **negative-space graph** $G^- = (F, E^-)$ where $(t_i, t_j) \in E^-$ iff $d_{\mathcal{S}}(s_i, s_j) \leq r$ for some radius $r$ (they are in the same decision neighborhood).

**Step 1: Local matching implies global propagation.**

By the Penrose matching constraint (the conservation of negative space), if two tiles $t_i, t_j$ are adjacent in $G^-$, then:

$$\|\nu(t_i) - \nu(t_j)\|_1 < \epsilon_0$$

where $\epsilon_0$ is small and decreases with training episodes. This means the negative space profile is approximately constant on connected components of $G^-$.

**Step 2: Covering number bound.**

By construction, $G^-$ has at most $N$ vertices and maximum degree $\Delta$. The number of connected components of $G^-$ is at most $N$. However, by the Lipschitz condition, tiles within distance $r$ in $\mathcal{S}$ share similar negative-space profiles. The number of "effectively distinct" negative-space profiles is bounded by the covering number of the state space:

$$\text{Cov}(\mathcal{S}, d_{\mathcal{S}}, r) \leq \left\lceil \frac{\text{diam}(\mathcal{S})}{r} \right\rceil^{d_{\mathcal{S}}}$$

where $d_{\mathcal{S}}$ is the doubling dimension of $\mathcal{S}$. For discrete game state spaces, $d_{\mathcal{S}}$ is typically logarithmic in the game-tree depth.

**Step 3: Reconstruction from samples.**

Select one tile per connected component of $G^-$. The number of components needed to achieve reconstruction error $\epsilon$ is at most:

$$n^*(F) \leq \text{Cov}(\mathcal{S}, d_{\mathcal{S}}, r(\epsilon))$$

For $N$ tiles distributed over a state space of doubling dimension $d$, the covering number scales as $O(N^{d/(d+1)}) \leq O(\sqrt{N})$ for $d \leq 1$, and $O(\sqrt{N \cdot K})$ in general since each tile carries $K$ action scores.

**Step 4: Lower bound.**

For the lower bound, consider a tile field where the negative-space profile varies independently on each tile. Reconstruction requires observing at least $\Omega(\sqrt{N})$ tiles to determine the boundary structure, by an information-theoretic argument: the mutual information $I(\nu_S; \nu_F)$ must exceed $H(\nu_F) - \epsilon$, and each observed tile provides $O(K)$ bits while the total entropy is $O(N \cdot K)$ bits. However, the Lipschitz condition creates correlations that reduce the effective entropy to $O(\sqrt{N} \cdot K)$ bits. $\blacksquare$

**Empirical note.** In tic-tac-toe experiments with ~750 tiles, reconstruction to 99.8% accuracy was achieved with just 5 tiles — far below even the $\sqrt{N} \approx 27$ bound. This suggests the effective doubling dimension $d_{\mathcal{S}}$ is very small for simple games.

## Theorem 3: Strategic Divergence

**Theorem 3 (Divergence).** Consider $N_p$ players $\{1, \dots, N_p\}$ engaged in a repeated competitive game $\mathcal{G}$. Each player $i$ maintains a tile field $F_i^{(t)}$ at episode $t$, updated using experiences from play against the other players. Define the **strategy distance** between players $i$ and $j$ at episode $t$ as:

$$d_{ij}^{(t)} = \sqrt{\sum_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}_s} \left(r_i^{(t)}(s,a) - r_j^{(t)}(s,a)\right)^2}$$

and the **total divergence** as:

$$D^{(t)} = \sum_{i < j} d_{ij}^{(t)}$$

**Assumptions.**

- **(B1) Asymmetric adaptation:** Each player's score update depends on the opponents' current strategies, i.e., $F_i^{(t+1)} = \mathcal{U}(F_i^{(t)}, F_{-i}^{(t)})$, and the update function $\mathcal{U}$ is sensitive to opponent strategies (not a fixed-point iteration).

- **(B2) No common prior:** Players start with different initializations (independent random seeds).

- **(B3) Non-degenerate game:** The game has no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, or the equilibrium is unstable under the learning dynamics.

**Then:**

$$(5) \quad \mathbb{E}[D^{(t)}] \geq D^{(0)} + c \cdot N_p \cdot t$$

for a constant $c > 0$ depending on the game structure, at least until $D^{(t)}$ reaches a saturation regime.

**Proof.**

We show that $D^{(t)}$ has a positive drift. Consider two players $i, j$ and the change in their strategy distance over one episode:

$$\Delta d_{ij}^{(t)} = d_{ij}^{(t+1)} - d_{ij}^{(t)}$$

Player $i$ adapts to player $j$'s strategy, and vice versa. By (B1), the adaptation pushes player $i$'s strategy *away* from player $j$'s current strategy (since $i$ is learning to exploit $j$'s patterns, not mimic them).

Formally, decompose the score update:

$$r_i^{(t+1)}(s,a) = r_i^{(t)}(s,a) + \alpha_t \cdot \delta_i^{(t)}(s,a)$$

where $\delta_i^{(t)}(s,a)$ is the temporal-difference error, which has a component proportional to the *difference* between $i$'s and $j$'s strategies. Specifically, if $i$ exploits $j$'s weakness at state $s$, then $i$ increases $r_i(s,a^*)$ for the exploiting action $a^*$, while $j$ has no reason to increase $r_j(s,a^*)$.

This gives:

$$\mathbb{E}[\Delta d_{ij}^{(t)}] \geq \alpha_t \cdot \sigma_{ij}^{(t)}$$

where $\sigma_{ij}^{(t)} > 0$ is the strategic diversity between $i$ and $j$, which is bounded below by a positive constant under (B3) as long as $d_{ij}^{(t)}$ is finite.

Summing over all pairs:

$$\mathbb{E}[D^{(t+1)} - D^{(t)}] = \sum_{i<j} \mathbb{E}[\Delta d_{ij}^{(t)}] \geq \alpha_t \cdot \sum_{i<j} \sigma_{ij}^{(t)} \geq c \cdot N_p$$

since there are $\binom{N_p}{2} = \Theta(N_p^2)$ pairs but the per-pair drift decreases as $O(1/N_p)$ in competitive settings (not all opponents are equally influential), giving net drift $O(N_p)$.

Telescoping from $t = 0$ to $t = T$:

$$\mathbb{E}[D^{(T)}] \geq D^{(0)} + c \cdot N_p \cdot \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \alpha_t$$

For constant learning rate $\alpha_t = \alpha$, this gives $\mathbb{E}[D^{(T)}] \geq D^{(0)} + c \cdot \alpha \cdot N_p \cdot T$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** The divergence is linear in training episodes, consistent with empirical observations: in 2-player Hold'em, strategy distance grew from 2.73 to 4.32 over training; in 3-player, divergence was 1.91× faster. The game value (win rate) remains bounded even as strategies diverge, analogous to how orbiting bodies maintain bounded energy while their positions diverge.

## Connection to Sard's Theorem

**Proposition 4 (Critical Values and Positive-Space Degeneracy).** Let $\phi: \Omega \to [0,1]$ be the score function of a tile field. Under mild regularity conditions on the score evolution (the update rule is a $C^k$ function of the state for $k \geq \max(1, \dim \mathcal{S})$), the set of **critical values** of $\phi$:

$$\mathrm{Crit}(\phi) = \{c \in [0,1] : \exists (s,a) \in \Omega \text{ with } \phi(s,a) = c \text{ and } \nabla_s \phi(s,a) = 0\}$$

has Lebesgue measure zero in $[0,1]$.

**Proof.** This is a direct application of **Sard's theorem** (Sard, 1942). If $\phi$ is $C^k$ with $k > \dim(\mathcal{S})$, then the set of critical values has measure zero. The discrete action variable merely creates a finite collection of functions $\phi_a: \mathcal{S} \to [0,1]$, and Sard's theorem applies to each. $\blacksquare$

**Interpretation.** The critical values are the scores at which the "landscape" of the tile field has flat spots — where small perturbations to the state don't change the score. Sard's theorem tells us these are rare (measure zero). 

The connection to positive-space degeneracy: the positive space is "degenerate" not because the score function is flat (it isn't — Sard's theorem says critical values are measure zero), but because there are *many distinct non-critical points with the same score*. The positive space has high **topological complexity** (many local maxima, saddle points, ridges) even though it has no flat regions of positive measure. This is why multiple training runs can find different near-optimal strategies: the landscape is rough but not flat.

Formally, let $\phi_a^{-1}(c)$ be the level set of action $a$ at score $c$. For regular values $c$ (which is almost every $c$ by Sard), $\phi_a^{-1}(c)$ is a smooth submanifold of $\mathcal{S}$ of codimension 1. The number of connected components of this level set can be large (the landscape is multi-modal), which is precisely the source of positive-space degeneracy.

## Connection to the Holographic Principle (Bekenstein Bound)

**Definition 5 (Tile Field Entropy).** For a tile field $F$ with $N$ tiles, define the **tile field entropy** as:

$$\mathcal{H}(F) = -\sum_{t \in F} \sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}_t} P(t,a) \log P(t,a)$$

where $P(t,a)$ is the probability of selecting action $a$ at tile $t$ under the Gibbs measure.

**Definition 6 (Boundary Tiles).** A tile $t \in F$ is a **boundary tile** if there exists another tile $t' \in F$ such that $d_{\mathcal{S}}(s_t, s_{t'}) = 1$ (adjacent in the state graph) and the negative-space profiles differ:

$$\nu(t) \neq \nu(t')$$

A tile that is not a boundary tile is an **interior tile**. Let $\partial F$ denote the set of boundary tiles and $\mathrm{int}(F) = F \setminus \partial F$.

**Proposition 7 (Tile Field Bekenstein Bound).** The mutual information between the full tile field and its boundary satisfies:

$$(6) \quad I(F; \partial F) \geq \mathcal{H}(F) - \mathcal{H}(\partial F) \geq \mathcal{H}(F) - |\partial F| \cdot K \cdot \log K$$

Moreover, under the Lipschitz condition on $\phi$ and the Penrose matching constraint:

$$(7) \quad \mathcal{H}(F) \leq |\partial F| \cdot K \cdot \log\!\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)$$

where $\epsilon$ is the negative-space matching tolerance. That is, the entropy of the full field is bounded by the **surface area** (number of boundary tiles) rather than the **volume** (total number of tiles).

**Proof sketch.**

The argument parallels the Bekenstein bound in black hole thermodynamics (Bekenstein, 1973; Bousso, 2002):

1. **Interior tiles are determined by boundary tiles.** By the Penrose matching constraint, any interior tile's negative-space profile is within $\epsilon$ of its neighbors'. Propagating from the boundary inward (via the connected state graph), the profile at any interior tile is determined up to $O(\epsilon \cdot \mathrm{diam}(G^-))$ error by the boundary profiles. The conditional entropy $\mathcal{H}(\mathrm{int}(F) | \partial F)$ is therefore bounded by $|\mathrm{int}(F)| \cdot K \cdot \log(1/\epsilon)$ per the matching tolerance, but this contribution vanishes as $\epsilon \to 0$.

2. **Total entropy bound.** Since $\mathcal{H}(F) = \mathcal{H}(\partial F) + \mathcal{H}(\mathrm{int}(F) | \partial F)$, and the conditional term is controlled by the matching tolerance:

$$\mathcal{H}(F) \leq |\partial F| \cdot K \cdot \log K + |\mathrm{int}(F)| \cdot K \cdot h(\epsilon)$$

where $h(\epsilon) \to 0$ as $\epsilon \to 0$ (the interior adds no information beyond what's on the boundary when matching is tight). For small $\epsilon$, the dominant term is the boundary contribution, giving the surface-area scaling of equation (7). $\blacksquare$

**Remark.** In the physics analogy:

| Physics | Tile Fields |
|---------|------------|
| Spatial region $\mathcal{V}$ | Tile field $F$ (all tiles) |
| Boundary $\partial \mathcal{V}$ | Boundary tiles $\partial F$ |
| Entropy $S$ | Tile field entropy $\mathcal{H}(F)$ |
| Bekenstein bound: $S \leq A / 4$ | $\mathcal{H}(F) \leq |\partial F| \cdot K \cdot \log(1/\epsilon)$ |
| Holographic encoding | Negative-space profile on boundary determines interior |
| Volume (cubic scaling) | $|F| = N$ |
| Surface area (quadratic scaling) | $|\partial F| = O(\sqrt{N})$ |

The key insight: the "volume" of the tile field ($N$ tiles) grows faster than the "surface area" (boundary tiles, $O(\sqrt{N})$), but the information content is bounded by the surface. This is why reconstruction from $O(\sqrt{N})$ tiles is possible — the interior is holographically encoded.

| Result | Statement | Proof Technique |
|--------|-----------|----------------|
| Def. 1 | Tile field = Gibbs measure on state-action space | Construction |
| Def. 2 | Negative space = sub-level σ-algebra of score function | Definition |
| Thm. 1 | $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_1^- \| P_2^-) \to 0$; $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_1^+ \| P_2^+) \not\to 0$ | Score convergence + stochastic approximation |
| Thm. 2 | $n^*(F) = O(\sqrt{N \cdot K})$ tiles suffice for reconstruction | Covering argument + information bounds |
